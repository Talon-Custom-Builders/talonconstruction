PLANNING & PRECONSTRUCTION

Before We Build Your Home, We Build It on Paper

A detailed proposal doesn't just show you a price — it shows you whether the builder actually understands your project. Here's how Talon builds every custom home on paper before anyone breaks ground.

September 14, 2026 Planning & Budget 4 min read

The short answer

A detailed custom home proposal should explain the scope of work, major construction phases and how the proposed cost relates to the work being performed. At Talon, the proposal also becomes part of the roadmap used to manage the project after construction begins.

A detailed construction proposal should do more than tell you what a project costs. It should show you that the builder understands what they are being asked to build.

At Talon Construction Company, preparing a proposal for a custom home or major remodel can take days. We study the architectural plans, think through how the work will actually be performed, and break the project down phase by phase and task by task.

That level of detail is intentional. We build the project on paper before we build it in the field.

A proposal is part of the construction process

It is easy to think of a proposal as the document that comes before the real work begins. We see it differently. For Talon, preparing the proposal is one of the first stages of the work itself.

We look at the drawings from the perspective of the people who will eventually have to execute them. That means considering the sequence of construction, the work our own crew will perform, where specialty trades will be involved, and how one phase needs to come together before the next can begin.

Then we document it. The result is not simply a few broad categories with a total at the bottom. It is a detailed outline of how we understand the project and what we believe it will take to build it properly.

Detail creates clarity before construction begins

Custom homes contain thousands of decisions. Some are large and obvious. Others are buried inside the work and may never be visible to the homeowner once construction is complete.

The more clearly a project can be defined before work begins, the better everyone understands what has been proposed. The homeowner can see what is included. The Talon team knows what has been committed to. Trade partners understand where their work fits into the larger project. And when construction begins, there is already a structure to work from.

That doesn't mean every possible condition can be predicted. Construction doesn't work that way. It means we've taken the time to understand as much as reasonably possible before asking a client to commit.

The proposal becomes a roadmap

One of the biggest benefits of preparing a project this way is that the proposal doesn't become irrelevant once it is accepted. Much of the organization established during estimating becomes the framework for managing the project.

The work has already been considered in phases. Tasks have been identified. Responsibilities have been considered. The sequence has begun to take shape. That gives the homeowner and the Talon team a common reference point as construction progresses.

For a substantial custom home or remodel, that matters. There are simply too many moving parts to manage well from memory, assumptions or a handful of broad line items.

It takes time. We think it should. Talon may spend days developing a proposal for a project we ultimately never build. That's part of the business. We're not interested in producing an artificially fast proposal simply to get a number in front of a prospective client. If we're going to tell someone what we believe their project will cost and how we intend to approach it, we want to have done the work behind that answer.

And for a homeowner comparing builders, the proposal can tell you something important before construction ever begins. Ask one simple question: Does this proposal show that the builder understands my project?

Not just the square footage. Not just the finishes. The project.

What should you look for in a construction proposal?

More pages do not automatically make a proposal better. Detail only matters when it creates understanding. A useful proposal should help you determine:

  • What work is actually included?
  • How has the builder organized the project?
  • Are the major phases understandable?
  • Can you see how the proposed price relates to the work?
  • Can the builder clearly explain the proposal when you have questions?
  • Does it appear that someone has genuinely studied your plans?

If the last answer is no, the number at the bottom of the page may not tell you as much as you think.

Good construction starts before the jobsite

The visible part of building comes later. The framing. The concrete. The cabinetry. The finishes.

Before any of that happens, there is planning. For Talon, that planning begins when we receive the drawings and start working through the project one piece at a time.

It's detailed work, and most of it isn't particularly glamorous. But it creates something we value highly: clarity. And clarity is a very good place to start a custom home.
